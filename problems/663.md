# Erdős Problem 663

**Database status checked 2026-08-30:** OPEN in `teorth/erdosproblems` (`data/problems.yaml`, current main).

**Experiment classification:** useful reformulation.

## Problem

For fixed `k >= 2`, let `q(n,k)` be the least prime which does not divide

`(n+1)(n+2)...(n+k)`.

Is

`q(n,k) < (1+o(1)) log n`

for all sufficiently large `n`?

The problem page records the easy bound `q(n,k) < (1+o(1)) k log n` and a probabilistic/CRT heuristic of Terence Tao for the conjectured coefficient `1`.

## Exact prime-allocation reformulation

Fix `y > k`. Suppose `q(n,k) > y`. Then every prime `p <= y` divides at least one of the `k` consecutive integers `n+1,...,n+k`.

For every prime `p > k`, it can divide **at most one** of those `k` integers: if it divided both `n+i` and `n+j`, then it would divide `j-i`, a nonzero integer of absolute value less than `k < p`, impossible.

Therefore every prime `k < p <= y` belongs to one and only one offset `j in {1,...,k}`. Define

`D_j = product of all primes p with k < p <= y that divide n+j`.

Then:

1. `D_j | n+j` for every `j`;
2. the `D_j` are pairwise coprime and squarefree;
3. `D_1 ... D_k = product_{k<p<=y} p`.

Conversely, these three properties imply that every prime `k<p<=y` divides one of the `n+j`; and every prime `p<=k` automatically divides one member of any block of `k` consecutive integers. Hence they imply `q(n,k)>y`.

So, for `y>k`, the condition `q(n,k)>y` is **equivalent** to partitioning the primes in `(k,y]` into `k` buckets whose squarefree products divide `k` consecutive integers.

Writing `n+j = D_j M_j`, the consecutiveness becomes the bounded-difference system

`D_j M_j - D_i M_i = j-i`

for every pair `i,j`.

This exposes the part discarded by the standard primorial-product argument: the prime mass is not merely distributed among `k` arbitrary integers of size about `n`; those integers must sit within an interval of length `k-1`.

## Why the easy coefficient k appears

Multiplying `D_j <= n+j` gives

`product_{k<p<=y} p <= product_{j=1}^k (n+j)`.

Taking logarithms and using the prime number theorem gives only

`y <= (k+o(1)) log n`,

which recovers the known easy coefficient `k`.

The conjectured coefficient `1` therefore cannot come from the product-size comparison alone. A proof has to exploit the **bounded-difference compatibility** among the `k` divisibility buckets (or an equivalent source of information).

## Prose version

Assume every small prime occurs somewhere in the block of consecutive integers. A prime larger than the block length cannot occur in two different positions, because then it would divide the small difference between those positions. Thus the larger small primes are forced to choose exactly one position each. Multiply the primes choosing each position: that product divides the integer occupying that position. The original problem is therefore a problem about partitioning the small primes into a fixed number of mutually exclusive buckets while forcing the resulting divisibility targets to differ from one another by only one, two, ..., `k-1`.

Merely multiplying all buckets forgets those small differences and gives the known factor `k`. Any route to the desired factor `1` must recover information lost by that multiplication.

## Novelty/status note

Tao's public heuristic already describes allocating the small primes among the `k` positions. This note does not claim novelty; it records the allocation as an exact iff reduction and isolates the bounded-difference equations as the information the elementary primorial argument throws away.
