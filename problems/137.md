# Erdős problem 137

**External status at attempt:** OPEN  
**Experiment status:** `useful-reformulation / proof-audit`  
**Complete solution obtained here:** no

Source statement: `google-deepmind/formal-conjectures`, `ErdosProblems/137.lean`.

## Statement

For `k >= 3`, can the product of `k` consecutive positive integers ever be powerful? Equivalently, must the product always contain some prime to exponent exactly one?

## Proof-audit result

A tempting reduction says that it should suffice to prove the case `k = 3`, on the intuition that adding more consecutive factors only creates additional prime constraints.

That reduction is invalid. Non-powerfulness is **not monotone under multiplication**: a prime that occurs to exponent one in a shorter product may acquire another copy from an added factor. Abstractly, `6` is not powerful while `6 * 6 = 36` is powerful. Thus a proof for every three-term consecutive product would not by itself imply the corresponding result for longer intervals.

## Safe local lemma

Let

`N = n(n+1)...(n+k-1)`.

If there is a prime `p > k-1` such that `p` divides exactly one term of the interval and does so only to the first power, then `N` is not powerful.

Indeed, a prime larger than the interval span cannot divide two distinct terms: if `p | (n+i)` and `p | (n+j)`, then `p | (i-j)`, impossible for `0 < |i-j| < p`. Hence its exponent in the full product is exactly its exponent in the unique divisible term. If that exponent is one, then `p | N` but `p^2 ∤ N`.

## Missing step

A complete solution would have to force such an exponent-one prime, or another obstruction to powerfulness, for **every** interval length `k >= 3` and every starting point. This attempt did not supply that universal bridge.
